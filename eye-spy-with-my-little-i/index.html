Flock Safety sells automated license plate reader cameras to police departments. Each camera photographs every vehicle that passes it — plate number, make, model, color, bumper stickers, roof racks — and uploads the data to a cloud platform where officers search it without a warrant, without a subpoena, and in Chesterfield County, without a written use policy. The Town of Cheraw has paid $688,660 for 54 cameras since 2020 and produced records showing 1,333 searches, 89.3 percent with no case number, resulting in two arrests and zero convictions. The Sheriff’s Office told a records requestor it had no Flock contracts; two days later it signed one. No agency in the county has produced a written policy governing who may search, for what reason, or what happens when the system is used without oversight. The United States Senate held a hearing. South Carolina’s Attorney General sent a letter. The same questions asked nationally were asked locally months earlier and remain unanswered. Published research and independent review

The Substack review of the published research: Researchers evaluated Atlantic City's installation of fixed plate readers at entrances and exits throughout the city. They found no reduction in overall violent crime. It adds that the research involved only one city and could not eliminate every other possible explanation. Of the federally supported evaluations: They did not find a significant reduction in general crime or motor-vehicle theft attributable to the technology. substack_flock_crime_claims.txt

Of Flock’s own white paper: There was no untreated comparison group, and the report measured whether Flock was reportedly involved, not whether it caused a case to be cleared or prevented a crime. substack_flock_crime_claims.txt

Of the multi-agency study behind Flock’s car-theft claim: The true effect, per the study's own statistics, could be nearly twice the headline number or well under half of it. Flock’s summary itself notes that "arrests had been rising before the deployment timeframe." yahoo_flock_11pct_cartheft.txt

The Institute for Justice on two towns with Flock: Take Springville, Alabama, which entered into a contract with Flock Safety in 2019. That year the property crime rate was 419 incidents per 100,000 people, according to FBI data compiled by the Crime Index. By 2023 it had more than tripled, to 1,510 per 100,000. Or consider Marietta, Georgia, which introduced Flock cameras in 2017. Local authorities have lavished praise on the system, but in the three years following its introduction the violent crime rate spiked from 349 to 489 incidents per 100,000 people. ij_flock_crime_drop.txt

It draws no causal line either way: None of this is to suggest the Flock cameras caused these observed spikes in crime, any more than the cameras caused the decreases in other cities cited by the technology's boosters. Its conclusion: While the benefits of ALPR technology remain unproven, the harms are well-documented. ij_flock_crime_drop.txt

From one town to the entire country

A Flock network is one agency’s camera system. A search of 1 network checks plates captured by Cheraw’s 54 cameras. A search across 6,194 networks queries the plate records of every vehicle that has passed any Flock camera in any participating agency across the United States.
